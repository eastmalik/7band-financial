**On screen:** WHAT'S ACTUALLY IN YOUR FILE · Personal information · Accounts · Public records · Inquiries · Three bureaus — Equifax, Experian, TransUnion — and they don't always match

**Say:** Your credit report has four parts. Personal information — names, addresses, employers. Accounts — every card and loan and how you've paid. Public records, like bankruptcies. ## Slide 26 — What You Can Challenge
**On screen:** WHAT THE LAW LETS YOU CHALLENGE · Inaccurate · Incomplete · Unverifiable · The bureau has to investigate — and fix or remove what can't be verified

**Say:** Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act you have the right to dispute information that's inaccurate, incomplete or can't be verified. When you do, the bureau has to investigate — usually within thirty days — and correct or remove what it can't verify. That's not a trick. That's the law working the way it's supposed to.

## Slide 27 — What Nobody Can Remove
**On screen:** WHAT NOBODY CAN REMOVE · Accurate negative information · It ages off on a schedule — most items 7 years, some bankruptcies 10 · What rebuilds it: on-time payments, lower balances, time

**Say:** Now the part most credit companies won't tell you. Accurate negative information — a late payment that really happened, an account that really went to collections — nobody can legally remove. It ages off on a schedule. Most items, seven years. Some bankruptcies, ten. Anybody who tells you they can delete accurate information is lying to you, and probably taking your money first. What actually rebuilds a file: paying on time, keeping balances low, and time. That's not exciting. It's true. And true is what works.

## Slide 32 — Level 4: The Engine
**On screen:** LEVEL 4: THE ENGINE · A properly designed, overfunded permanent life policy can build cash value you can borrow against. · The truth: it has real costs · Loans accrue interest · Underfunding can lapse a policy · It's for people who actually want the insurance

**Say:** Level 4 is the engine. A permanent life insurance policy, designed and funded for cash value, can build a pool of money you can borrow against while you're alive — and it carries a death benefit for your family. Banks own life insurance on their own books too, within the limits their regulators set. But here's what most presentations leave out. It has real costs inside it. Loans accrue interest. If it's underfunded or over-borrowed, a policy can lapse — and that can cost you the coverage and create a tax bill. And it's for people who actually want the insurance, not just the account. If someone shows you this without showing you the costs, you already have your answer.

## Slide 33 — Choose Your Engine
**On screen:** CHOOSE YOUR ENGINE · WHOLE LIFE — The Paladin · Guarantees, fixed premium, steady dividends, contractual growth floor · INDEXED UNIVERSAL LIFE — The Rogue · Market-linked upside, capped gains, floor under index losses, flexible premiums · Neither is the winner. The situation and the design decide.

**Say:** There are two main engines. Whole life is the Paladin — guarantees, fixed premiums, dividends that aren't guaranteed but have a long track record, a contractual floor. It never surprises you. Indexed universal life is the Rogue — growth linked to a market index, with a cap on the upside, a floor under index losses, and flexible premiums. It trades rigidity for potential — and that flexibility cuts both ways. Neither one is the winner. Your situation and the design decide.

## Slide 34 — Warning: The MEC Trap
**On screen:** WARNING: THE MEC TRAP · Fund too much, too fast in the first 7 years and the policy becomes a Modified Endowment Contract · Loans and withdrawals get taxed differently — and can be penalized · A great concept with a bad design still fails.

**Say:** One warning. If you put too much money in, too fast, in the first seven years, the policy fails a test called the 7-pay test and becomes a Modified Endowment Contract — an MEC. Once that happens, loans and withdrawals get taxed differently, and you can owe a penalty if you're under 59½. The concept was great. The design failed it. That's why this level isn't a do-it-yourself level.

The Transfer: choosing your beneficiaries on purpose instead of by default. And the Generational Tree: teaching the next generation the map, so they start at Level 3 instead of Level 0.
